# Impact statement

## The problem

Most musicians learn without a teacher. Lessons, a producer and studio time cost money, so honest feedback on your
own music — what's working, what's missing, how you're improving — mostly goes to people who can already pay for it.
That gap follows income and geography.

## What this does about it

A free second pair of ears, on the recordings people already make (a phone memo is enough):

- **Plain-language feedback** on one recording: key, chords, form, groove, who plays when, energy and mood, with ideas
  to try first, built on what the track leaves out.
- **Patterns across recordings**: what's there in all of a musician's tracks and what none of them has yet, and how
  that changes from session to session (Your style).
- **Next to music they admire**: what they already share, and what theirs has that yours doesn't.
- **For teachers and community programmes**: groups that see only the work members choose to share, with what's there
  and not there across the group — material for a lesson.
- **Phone first**: record in the browser (iPhone included), keep recordings, tag them, analyse when ready.

## Rights, not extraction

- Audio sent for analysis is deleted once analysed; recordings a musician keeps are playable only by them.
- Nothing uploaded trains any model; the optional AI sees numbers only, never audio or file names.
- Music discovery points to artists with 20–10,000 listeners, away from the few names that streaming concentrates on.
- The style and instrument models (MTG-UPF) are CC BY-NC-SA 4.0, non-commercial: they fit a free, educational tool.

## How sure it is (measured, not claimed)

Every deploy runs the self-test on music with known answers — generated tracks and real recordings whose tempo, key and
line-up the musicians confirmed — and saves the results; the About page shows the last run. Known limits, stated on every
report: the key is right about 70–80% of the time on real music (usually the slip is the relative key), tempo can come
out at half or double, chords are simplified.

## What we measure (and what we don't)

Shown on the About page, as totals only, with counts under 10 hidden:

| Measure | What it suggests |
|---|---|
| Tracks analysed | reach |
| Musicians who came back with a second track | it was worth returning to |
| Share of rated ideas marked helpful (👍 vs 👎) | the feedback is useful |
| Recordings kept, groups | use beyond a one-off |

Not collected: who, what music, listening behaviour, anything sold or shared.

## A pilot, to turn this into evidence

1. Recruit 10–20 musicians without a teacher (a community music programme or a school club is the easiest route), and
   one facilitator with a group.
2. Four weeks: each records or uploads at least one track a week, rates the ideas, and the group shares one track each
   per week.
3. Before and after: a short questionnaire (confidence describing their own music; how often they get feedback;
   whether they tried anything new), plus what the app already records — ideas marked helpful, and the change between
   their first and latest sessions in Your style.
4. Report it with the limits: small sample, self-selected, no control group.

## Numbers to source before quoting any

Find these from primary sources (surveys by music education bodies, instrument makers' published studies, national
statistics) and cite them; don't use figures that can't be traced:

- the share of musicians who are self-taught or learn mostly online;
- the cost of private lessons, and how many people stop learning an instrument early;
- the share of independent artists' streams compared with the top artists' (for the discovery point).

## Not done yet

- **Languages**: the interface and reports are English only. Translating the interface is the next step for reach.
- **Offline**: analysis needs a connection; recording needs one only to save.
